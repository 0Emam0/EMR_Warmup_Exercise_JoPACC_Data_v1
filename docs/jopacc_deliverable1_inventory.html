01 — How the two-year scope should be read

Both readings give the same 34 files

The stated window is 14 September 2024 to 14 September 2026. Those dates fall mid-month, and JoPACC never publishes a report covering a partial period, so the window has to be snapped outward to whole periods. There are two defensible ways to do that, and the interesting result is that on this particular window they do not disagree.

InterpretationMonthlyQuarterlyYearlyTotal
By reporting period — the period the data describes overlaps the window 2024-09 → 2026-08Q3 2024 → Q2 20262024, 202534
By publication date — the report was released inside the window 2024-09 → 2026-08Q3 2024 → Q2 20262024, 202534

Checking this against JoPACC's publication dates rather than assuming it: the August 2024 monthly went out on 11 September 2024, three days before the window opens, so it falls out under both readings. The August 2026 monthly went out on 13 September 2026, one day before the window closes, so it falls in under both. Q2 2024 was published 16 July 2024 and is out; Q3 2024 was published 16 October 2024 and is in. The 2023 annual report predates the window and the 2026 annual report will not exist until roughly March 2027, leaving 2024 and 2025.

Recommendation: define the scope by reporting period anyway. The two readings agree here, but that is a coincidence of where the window boundaries happen to fall, and it will stop being true the moment the window moves. Reporting period is also the more stable key: it is a property of the data, whereas a publication date belongs to the file and gets overwritten when JoPACC re-issues one — which, as the next section shows, has happened five times in this corpus.

Either way, the 34 files you uploaded are exactly the right 34. Nothing is missing and nothing extra is present.

Edge periods worth naming explicitly

  • Q3 2024 covers 1 July to 30 September 2024. Only the last seventeen days fall inside the window. It is included because the quarter cannot be subdivided.
  • The 2024 annual report covers all of calendar 2024, of which roughly three and a half months precede the window. Same reasoning.
  • Q3 2026 and the September 2026 monthly do not exist yet. The quarter has not closed and the month is still running as of today. Their absence is expected, not a gap.
  • The 2026 annual report will not appear until around March 2027.

02 — Completeness and integrity

All 34 files open cleanly, report a sensible page count, and carry an internal reporting period that agrees with their filename. No file is byte-identical to another, and no reporting period appears twice.

One detection failure, not a data problem. On the Q1 2025 cover, OCR reads the quarter label as QI 2025 — the digit 1 resolving as a capital I. The file is correct; the reader was not. Period parsing in the pipeline has to normalise the usual OCR confusions (I/1, O/0, S/5) before matching, and flag any file whose parsed period disagrees with its filename rather than silently trusting either.

Every uploaded file is the canonical current version

All 34 filenames match the download links on JoPACC's site character for character, numeric suffixes included. The six files carrying a _0 or _1 suffix are not stray local copies — that is genuinely how JoPACC serves them, the result of uploading a file more than once under the same name. You have the current published version of every report in the window.

Five reports have been silently replaced since first publication

JoPACC stores each upload in a month-stamped folder, so comparing the folder a file is served from against the date shown next to it exposes replacements the listing page does not advertise. The displayed date always stays at the original publication; the file behind it can be newer.

  • 2024 annual report — listed as 26 March 2025, but served from the July 2026 folder, and the PDF's own modification timestamp is 30 July 2026. Sixteen months later. This is the most consequential one, because an annual report being rebuilt that long after release suggests restated figures rather than a cosmetic fix.
  • November 2025 monthly — listed as 11 December 2025, served from the January 2026 folder, built 19 January 2026 through Adobe rather than PowerPoint. It contains 61 embedded images where its neighbours contain roughly 1,500, so it was rebuilt from scratch, not re-exported.
  • January 2026 monthly — listed as 12 February 2026, served from the April 2026 folder.
  • February 2026 monthly — listed as 17 March 2026, served from the April 2026 folder. Together with January, re-issued as a pair on 9 April 2026.
  • July 2026 monthly — listed as 12 August 2026, but the PDF was built 30 August 2026, eighteen days later, and is served under a _0 suffix.

Consequence for the pipeline. A file's publication date cannot be trusted as a version identifier. The inventory layer should record the content hash, the internal creation timestamp and the internal modification timestamp for every PDF, and treat a changed hash under an unchanged filename as a re-issue that forces reprocessing rather than a cache hit.

It is also worth re-extracting the 2024 annual report against the 2025 one once both are processed. If the 2024 figures were restated in the July 2026 rebuild, the 2025 report's year-on-year comparisons are the place that will show it.

04 — The finding that shapes the whole build

Half the corpus has no text in it at all

Seventeen of the 34 files contain essentially no extractable text. An 81-page monthly report returns around 275 characters — barely more than its cover. Every heading, axis label and data value has been converted to vector outlines on export, so there is nothing for a text extractor to find. Rendered to screen they look identical to the readable files; the difference is invisible until you try to read them programmatically.

The split is almost perfectly chronological. Everything published up to and including the August 2025 monthly is outlined. Everything from the September 2025 monthly onward carries real text. JoPACC evidently changed an export setting in autumn 2025.

This is where the accuracy risk lives. A plain OCR pass over these pages reads the headings correctly and misses the numbers. The values that matter sit in white type on grey bars and in amber type inside ring charts, and default Tesseract settings discard both.

Testing on a representative page established what actually works. Rendering at 200 dpi and running Tesseract in sparse-text mode across three separately isolated colour layers — dark type, light type, amber type — recovers the bar values that a single pass loses. The amber figures additionally need connected-component filtering to strip the surrounding ring, which otherwise reads as punctuation and suppresses the number entirely. With that in place the percentage KPI on the test page resolves at 94 % confidence, against a complete miss before.

Because both paths return words with bounding boxes, text-bearing and outlined files can feed a single downstream model: values are matched to their axis labels by x-position, not by reading order. On the test page the three bar values align to Feb/Mar/Apr within nine pixels.

05 — Three layouts, not one

The three types are significantly different in layout while sharing a common analytical vocabulary: the same four payment systems, the same volume-and-value metric pair, the same on-us/off-us and money-transfer/purchase breakdowns. They need three extraction strategies feeding one data model, not one strategy.

What the monthly deck contains

Section divider slides carry their titles as real text, which makes them a reliable way to segment the deck. In the current 87-page layout the sections are: Systems Summary (p3); CliQ, the instant payment system for bank transfers (p9); JoMoPay, the mobile payments switch (p25); CliQ–JoMoPay interoperability (p41); instant payments in Jordan combined (p54); eFAWATEERcom bill presentment and payment (p60); Automated Clearing House (p72); and the Electronic Cheque Clearing Unit (p78).

The four 2024 files use an earlier seven-section layout with dividers at 3, 9, 25, 41, 54, 66 and 72. The structures agree up to page 54 and diverge after it, which is consistent with the eight-section layout being an expansion rather than a reshuffle. Section boundaries must be discovered per file from the divider slides, never hard-coded.

Two limitations to record now rather than discover later.

System identity is frequently carried by a logo image, not by text. A slide headed “Summary” with a CliQ logo returns only the word Summary. The system a slide belongs to has to be inherited from its enclosing section, which is another reason the divider-based segmentation matters.

The annual reports plot twelve-month trend lines with no data labels — only an axis. Those monthly series cannot be read without estimating from pixel positions, which the brief rules out. The pipeline should extract the labelled totals and commentary from those pages and log the unlabelled series as a known, deliberate omission. The same figures are available at full precision from the monthly reports anyway.

06 — Verification against the source

Every claim in this document about publication dates, filenames and file replacement was checked against JoPACC's publications listing on 15 September 2026. The listing holds 102 payment system reports in total; the 34 in scope are a contiguous and complete slice of it.

Nothing material is left open. Two smaller points are worth carrying forward:

  • Re-check before each production run. Because replacements are invisible on the listing page, the only reliable detection is comparing content hashes. Running the inventory layer against a fresh download is the check.
  • The 2024 annual report is the one to watch. It is the single file where a replacement is likely to have changed published figures rather than formatting.
